    Practices

    Meditations and calming techniques

    Eleven practices you can do at home, from the Taoist orbit and Buddhist metta to mindful eating and slow walking. What each one is, where it comes from, what it gives and how to do it.

    Before you begin

    How to choose a practice

    Four rules that answer half the questions before the first session.

    • Where to start

      With the body scan or shamatha: they forgive mistakes and need no imagery. Ten minutes a day is enough to see how attention behaves.

    • How often

      Short and regular beats long and rare. Three or four times a week already makes a difference you can notice.

    • How long to wait

      The first shifts usually come after two to four weeks: first it becomes easier to notice wandering, then quiet itself deepens.

    • What not to expect

      Quiet does not arrive on demand. Practice is training in returning, not an exam in having an empty head, and getting distracted is normal.

    On evidence

    How we describe effects

    In these descriptions we keep three things apart: what the tradition says, what a person feels in practice, and what research actually confirms. Where data are thin, we say so, even if the practice is beautiful and old.

    None of the practices on this page is a treatment. Meditation can lower stress and improve mood and attention, but with anxiety, depression, chronic pain and other conditions it works alongside a doctor and therapy, not instead of them.

    01

    Classical practices

    Traditions in which attention and breath settle into a steady form.

    01 / Taoism, qigong

    Microcosmic Orbit

    A closed circuit of attention: up the spine and down the front of the body.

    The microcosmic orbit, xiao zhou tian in Chinese, is one of the foundational practices of Taoist inner alchemy, neidan. Attention travels a closed loop: up the back along the Governing vessel, du mai, and down the front of the body along the Conception vessel, ren mai. The two channels join into a ring, hence the word orbit. The breath sets the rhythm of that movement but does not become an object of control.

    The tradition traces the practice to Taoist alchemical texts: early mentions of the circle go back to the Zhouyi cantong qi (2nd century), and a fuller description appears in Zhang Boduan's Wuzhen pian (1075). In its classical form the orbit was a preparatory stage: before working with subtle states, the practitioner learned to carry attention through the body without breaks. The practice reached the West through Charles Luk's book of 1970 and Mantak Chia's of 1983, and in its popular form it was considerably simplified.

    What it looks like in a hall or at home: an upright back, relaxed shoulders, the tongue resting lightly against the palate. Attention gathers below the navel, in the lower dantian, and from there the circle begins. Usually one makes nine, eighteen or thirty-six rounds, then simply sits in silence. Sensations along the orbit are mostly warm and vague: tingling in the back, heaviness in the belly, coolness on the face. They are not the goal but a side effect.

    How it works

    The mechanics here are simple and entirely earthy. Attention is tied to a long route, so the mind has less room to fall into a random thought: it has a job. The breath slows to five or six cycles a minute, and such breathing by itself shifts the autonomic balance towards the parasympathetic side.

    There are no clinical trials of the microcosmic orbit specifically. Studies of qigong exist, but their quality is often poor, and the notions of qi and meridians have no confirmed anatomical basis. This does not cancel the effect: relaxation, slower breathing and attention training work regardless of how they are explained. But the explanation is worth holding as the language of a tradition, not as physiology.

    How to do it

    1. Sit comfortably with a straight back, hands on the thighs or folded below the navel.
    2. Relax the shoulders and jaw, rest the tongue lightly against the palate, take three quiet breaths.
    3. Gather attention two or three centimetres below the navel and stay there for a few breaths.
    4. On the inhale, move attention from the perineum up the spine, through the back of the head, to the crown.
    5. On the exhale, move it down the face, chest and belly, back to the starting point.
    6. Make nine, eighteen or thirty-six circles, then sit in silence for another minute.

    Signs it is working

    • By the third or fourth round the route holds itself, without inner reminders.
    • An even warmth or coolness appears along the back, rather than isolated flashes.
    • After practice the body feels gathered and the head clear, without heaviness.

    Time: 15-20 minutes Posture: seated Level: beginner

    02 / Theravada Buddhism

    Metta

    Loving-kindness: a silent wish of well-being for yourself and for others.

    Metta, or loving-kindness meditation, comes from the Theravada Buddhist tradition. It opens the four immeasurables, the brahmaviharas: metta, karuna (compassion), mudita (sympathetic joy) and upekkha (equanimity). The core gesture is simple: you wish well-being, starting with yourself and gradually widening the circle.

    The earliest known statement of the practice is the Karaniya Metta Sutta in the Sutta Nipata (Sn 1.8), a short text on how to live for one who wants peace. The systematic description comes in the Visuddhimagga by Buddhaghosa, a fifth-century manual: there metta is laid out step by step, with an order of people and with the mistakes one easily falls into.

    The traditional order is not accidental: yourself first, then someone dear, then a neutral person, then someone difficult, and finally all beings. One starts with oneself not out of egoism but because without warmth towards oneself the wish for others quickly becomes a polite formula. The formula itself is short: may I be calm, may I be well, may I be at ease.

    How it works

    What works here is repeated intention. You say a short phrase, hold a particular person in mind and notice what happens in the body: warmth in the chest, a lump in the throat, a wish to be distracted. The body often answers before the words do, and that is the surest sign the practice is running.

    Metta has been studied more than many other practices. In Fredrickson and colleagues (2008, 139 people, waitlist comparison), seven weeks of practice produced more positive emotions, higher life satisfaction and fewer depressive symptoms. A meta-analysis by Zeng and colleagues (2015) reports small to moderate effects with high heterogeneity, while the review by Goyal and colleagues (2014) finds evidence insufficient for improved mood. Put simply: the direction is there, the size is modest and depends on regularity.

    How to do it

    1. Sit or lie down comfortably and take a few quiet breaths.
    2. Direct the wish to yourself: may I be calm, may I be well.
    3. Bring to mind someone close to you and wish the same for them.
    4. Then a neutral person, a neighbour or a passer-by.
    5. Finally someone difficult, as far as this is possible without forcing yourself.
    6. Widen the wish to all people and beings around you.

    Signs it is working

    • The phrase stops being text and begins to register in the body, at least with one person.
    • During the day you notice irritation before you have already answered it.
    • A difficult person comes to mind without an instant tightening in the chest.

    Time: 10-20 minutes Posture: seated or lying down Level: beginner

    03 / Buddhism, brahmaviharas

    Mudita

    Sympathetic joy: delight in another person's good fortune, trainable like attention.

    Mudita is the third of the four immeasurables, sympathetic joy. It is the capacity to be genuinely glad when things go well for someone else: their success, health, calm, a small piece of luck. The tradition considers it the hardest of the four, because it meets envy directly.

    The Visuddhimagga names two enemies for each brahmavihara. Mudita's far enemy is envy, issa. Its near enemy, the one disguised as virtue, is pahasa, feigned delight: a person performs joy for another in order to look good. Telling these two states apart is half of the practice.

    The traditional order is the same as in metta: someone dear, a neutral person, a difficult one, all beings. But it is easier to begin not with people but with simple examples: a child learning to walk, a stranger's dog finding a home. The task is to let joy happen rather than construct it out of politeness.

    How it works

    Mudita changes how the mind appraises an event. Another's success is usually read as a threat to one's own standing. Sympathetic joy offers another frame: good that happened nearby is good in general, and it is not necessarily taken from you.

    There is almost no research on mudita: individual studies and reviews mention it within compassion programmes, but we found no randomised trials of sympathetic joy as such. The neighbouring territory is well studied: the link between envy and burnout, and the quality of relationships. Treat the practice as training in attention and honesty with yourself, not as a treatment.

    How to do it

    1. Recall someone whose happiness is easy for you to enjoy and think: I am glad for you.
    2. Notice where this joy registers in the body: chest, belly, face.
    3. Let the feeling be there without amplifying it.
    4. Widen the wish: may good things come to everyone I care about.
    5. If envy appears, note it calmly and return to the wish.

    Signs it is working

    • Another's good fortune can be recalled without an inner bargain.
    • Envy is noticed as envy, not as 'objective injustice'.
    • Gladness for another lasts longer than a few seconds.

    Time: 10-15 minutes Posture: seated Level: intermediate

    04 / Indian tradition

    Shamatha

    One attention, one object, and a calm return each time the mind wanders.

    Shamatha, or calm abiding, is the practice of one-pointed attention. You choose a single object, most often the breath, and learn to return to it every time the mind moves away. Wandering is not a mistake here; it is part of the work, because the returning is what trains stability.

    The classical scheme of nine stages is associated with Asanga's Shravakabhumi (4th century) and with Kamalashila's Bhavanakrama (around 792-794), where the path of attention is drawn with an elephant and a monkey: from complete wandering to even abiding. The nine stages are a map, not an exam: most practitioners live between the third and the fifth.

    The object can be the breath at the nose, the movement of the belly, a candle flame, a sound or an image. The breath is the most convenient: it is always with you and needs no props. The object shapes the character of the practice, not its essence: hold, notice the departure, return.

    How it works

    Shamatha trains what psychology calls sustained attention and inhibitory control: the ability to hold a goal and not react to a distraction. Repeating the return literally paves a short path to the object, and in time it fires by itself.

    The research is cautious. A three-month retreat in MacLean and colleagues (2010) improved sustained attention, but the sample was small. Reviews show small and heterogeneous effects, and the critical appraisal by Van Dam and colleagues (2018) reminds us that some celebrated results do not replicate. The practice is still useful, but as training, not as a pill.

    How to do it

    1. Sit with a straight back and let the eyes close or soften.
    2. Choose one anchor: the feeling of air at the nose or the movement of the belly.
    3. Keep attention in that one place, without controlling the breath.
    4. When you notice the mind has gone, return gently, without reproach.
    5. Count the returns, not the minutes.

    Signs it is working

    • You notice attention leaving within seconds, not minutes.
    • The breath stays even even when the mind wanders: the panic about it goes away.
    • Twenty minutes pass without an inner clock.

    Time: 10-25 minutes Posture: seated Level: beginner

    05 / Theravada Buddhism

    Vipassana

    Observing what is happening, without choosing and without judging.

    Vipassana translates as insight: seeing things as they are. Instead of one object, attention moves freely through bodily sensations, warmth, coolness, tingling, pressure, vibration. Whatever appears is noted and released.

    The root of the practice is the Satipatthana Sutta (MN 10), the discourse on the four foundations of mindfulness. In its modern form vipassana was spread by ten-day courses: S. N. Goenka held the first in Mumbai on 3-13 July 1969, continuing the line of U Ba Khin. The format is demanding: ten days of silence, long hours of sitting, early rising, no reading or writing.

    The home version is gentler: short sessions in which you watch sensations arise and pass. The point is not to find a pleasant state but to see impermanence. What looked like a monolith falls apart into changing pieces, and that changes your relationship to your own reactions.

    How it works

    Vipassana trains what is called decentering: the ability to look at an experience from the side without fusing with it. Attention to changing bodily signals strengthens interoception, and the habit of noting and releasing reduces the stickiness of emotions.

    The evidence base is uneven. Chiesa's review (2010) found about seven studies of vipassana, mostly of low quality. The meta-analysis by Goyal and colleagues (2014) reports moderate effects for anxiety and depression, but it covers secular MBSR and MBCT programmes, not vipassana as such. Separately, Lindahl and colleagues (2017) show that some practitioners experience unpleasant effects, and intensive retreats are not safe for everyone.

    How to do it

    1. Sit comfortably and close your eyes.
    2. Begin with sensations in the belly and chest.
    3. Move attention through the body, noting whatever is noticeable.
    4. Do not linger on the pleasant, and do not push away the unpleasant.
    5. Notice the moment when a sensation changes or disappears.

    Signs it is working

    • A sensation can be noted before it has turned into a thought.
    • An unpleasant experience ends, and you notice that instead of falling into it.
    • The urge to react appears but is not necessarily acted out.

    Time: 15-45 minutes Posture: seated Level: intermediate

    06 / Yogic tradition

    Chakra meditation

    Seven centres, seven lights, and a slow movement of attention from the base upward.

    Chakra meditation works with an image: along the spine you picture seven luminous centres, from the base of the body to the crown. Tradition gives each one a colour, a sound and a quality. The exact shade matters less than the practice itself: attention to the body and to its states.

    The chakra system formed in Indian tantric literature. An extended description of six centres is given in Purnananda's Shat-chakra-nirupana (1577), and the West received it through John Woodroffe's The Serpent Power (1919). The familiar seven chakras with rainbow colours took shape in the twentieth century, in theosophical and New Age circles, and have no direct relation to the medieval text.

    Movement usually goes upward. You pause at each centre, imagine a soft glow and observe what happens in the body. If no image appears, it is enough to place attention in that area and feel it. In this practice the chakras remain a language of description, not anatomy.

    How it works

    Two mechanisms work here psychologically: guided imagery and body attention. The image gives the mind a support, and moving through the zones in sequence keeps attention from spreading out. Any body-oriented visualisation, from the body scan to warmth practices, produces a similar effect.

    It should be said plainly: chakras do not describe anatomical structures, and there is no reproducible physiological evidence for them. Research on the practice itself is scarce and samples are small. That does not make the practice meaningless: it works as an exercise in attention and imagination, while traditional explanations remain the language of tradition.

    How to do it

    1. Sit upright, close your eyes and take a few breaths.
    2. Place attention at the base of the body and imagine a warm light.
    3. Move slowly to the next centre with each exhale.
    4. Rest at each centre for three to five breaths.
    5. Reach the crown and imagine the light extending beyond the body.
    6. Return attention to the breath and sit quietly.

    Signs it is working

    • The image holds at each centre for several breaths without falling apart.
    • Warmth, heaviness or pulsing appear in the areas, different in different zones.
    • After the passage the body feels even and the head quieter.

    Time: 15-20 minutes Posture: seated Level: intermediate

    07 / Kriya yoga

    Spinal breathing

    Inhale upward along the spine, exhale downward, without strain or holds.

    Spinal breathing is a foundational kriya yoga practice. Attention travels with the breath along the spinal column: on the inhale from the base upward to the point between the eyebrows, on the exhale back down. The movement happens more in imagination and subtle sensation than in muscle.

    The line of transmission runs from Lahiri Mahasaya to Sri Yukteswar and on to Paramahansa Yogananda, who described it in Autobiography of a Yogi (1946). The word kriya here means action: the breath and the attention perform the same work, and that is the whole point.

    This is not belly breathing and not breath retention. The breath stays even and quiet. The practice is often done before main meditation, to gather attention and warm the body: ten or fifteen cycles, then a short pause and silence.

    How it works

    The main physiological contribution here comes from the breath. Slow breathing at about six cycles a minute increases heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity: this was shown by Bernardi and colleagues (2001) and confirmed by Zaccaro and colleagues' 2018 review of parasympathetic markers.

    There are no trials of spinal breathing itself. The physiology of slow breathing is studied; the specific kriya is not. That is an honest frame: relaxation and collectedness are expected, and the stronger promises of the tradition we do not confirm.

    How to do it

    1. Sit with a straight back and relax the shoulders and jaw.
    2. Place attention at the base of the spine.
    3. On the inhale, move it up along the spine to the point between the eyebrows.
    4. On the exhale, move it down the same path, back to the base.
    5. Breathe slowly, without holds, for ten or fifteen cycles.

    Signs it is working

    • The breath slows by itself by the fifth cycle, without a command.
    • Attention travels the whole route without dropping into thought halfway.
    • After practice the back holds straight and the head is clear.

    Time: 5-10 minutes Posture: seated, before meditation Level: beginner

    08 / Hatha yoga

    Shambhavi Mudra

    A soft gaze into the space ahead and attention at the centre of the forehead.

    Shambhavi mudra, also called bhairavi mudra, comes from hatha yoga and is connected with the ajna chakra. The eyes stay open, but the gaze does not catch on objects: it is gently unfocused and directed into the space ahead, while attention is gathered between the eyebrows.

    Classical descriptions appear in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika (4.35-37) and the Gheranda Samhita (chapter III). There the mudra is a way to hold gaze and mind at one point, a step towards contemplation. In the modern world it was popularised by Sadhguru as Shambhavi Mahamudra: that is a composite practice with breath and movements, not literally the classical mudra.

    From the outside it looks like quiet contemplation. Inside, the work is subtle: the eyes are relaxed, the mind is not busy analysing what it sees, and attention rests in one point. Many describe the state as clear and quiet at the same time.

    How it works

    With an unfocused gaze the visual system stops scanning details, and the stream of commentary that usually follows them quietens with it. The point between the eyebrows adds a bodily anchor: attention is held not only by the eyes but by a sensation in the forehead.

    Research on the mudra is sparse. Small studies of the Isha programme, where this practice is part of Shambhavi Mahamudra, report lower stress and better sleep (Peterson et al., 2017; Upadhyay et al., 2022), but samples are small, authors are often affiliated with the organisation, and independent replications are absent. We offer this as a cautious reference, not as proof.

    How to do it

    1. Sit comfortably with a straight back and the chin slightly lowered.
    2. Without closing the eyes, direct the gaze forward and slightly upward.
    3. Soften the focus, without crossing the eyes toward the nose.
    4. Gather attention at the point between the eyebrows.
    5. Breathe calmly. If the eyes tire, close them and rest.

    Signs it is working

    • The gaze stays unfocused for a minute or more without strain.
    • Thoughts run quieter, but everything remains visible.
    • After practice the eyes do not ache or water.

    Time: 5-15 minutes Posture: seated Level: intermediate

    09 / Mindfulness practices

    Mindfulness in movement

    Walking in which the step matters, not the destination.

    Meditation in movement carries attention off the cushion and into ordinary life. The canonical version is simple: you walk and you know that you walk. The most accessible format is slow walking along a straight line, where what matters is not where you are going but how the foot meets the floor.

    In the Satipatthana Sutta (MN 10) mindfulness in walking appears as one of the postures: sitting, standing, walking, lying down. In Zen the practice became kinhin, walking between sittings, and in the Thai forest tradition slow walking is trained separately, with precise speed and attention to the foot.

    The home version needs nothing: five metres of a room, a corridor, a path in a park. The same principle works in any activity: washing dishes, climbing stairs, the walk to the station. Choose one action and do it a little slower than usual, staying entirely inside it.

    How it works

    Walking adds rhythm to attention. The repeating cycle of the step stabilises the state: it is no accident that we start pacing the room when we cannot sit still. A slow tempo leaves time to notice transitions: heel, foot, shift of weight.

    The data here concern walks rather than walking meditation. A ninety-minute walk in nature reduced rumination and activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex (Bratman et al., 2015, 38 people). A 2022 meta-analysis shows that nature walks are associated with lower depression and anxiety. The contribution of mindfulness itself is not separated in these studies, so we should be careful with conclusions.

    How to do it

    1. Choose a level stretch of five to ten metres.
    2. Walk slower than usual, with no goal of arriving.
    3. Notice in turn: heel, foot, the shift of weight.
    4. At the turn, stop, register the movement and walk back.
    5. At the end, stand for a minute and feel the whole body.

    Signs it is working

    • The step slows by itself, without counting.
    • Thoughts come, but the body stays in the field of attention.
    • After ten minutes the head is clearer than before.

    Time: 10-20 minutes Posture: outdoors or at home Level: beginner

    10 / Mindfulness practices

    Mindful eating

    One meal, no screen, full attention on taste.

    Mindful eating is one of the most accessible practices, because eating happens every day. The point is to eat not on autopilot: to notice the look, the smell, the texture, the taste and the moment when fullness arrives.

    In the Buddhist monastic tradition the meal was part of the practice: food was regarded as medicine and as a reminder of dependence on others. In secular form the raisin exercise appeared in Jon Kabat-Zinn's stress reduction programme (from 1979). Jean Kristeller then built a separate programme on it, MB-EAT, in the late 1990s: twelve sessions on hunger, satiety and emotional eating.

    The classic exercise is to eat one raisin in five minutes. It may seem strange, yet that slowing down shows how many layers of experience we usually skip. At the table it is enough to put the phone away and eat the first three minutes in silence.

    How it works

    Satiety is not only the stomach: signals come from the stretching of its walls, from hormones and from attention to food itself. When we eat fast and with a screen, those signals are lost. Slowing down brings them back into view.

    The review by Godfrey and colleagues (2015) gathered 19 studies: the between-group difference for overeating was about g = -0.70, but heterogeneity was very high (I-squared around 90%). The MB-EAT randomised trial (2014) showed fewer binge episodes. For weight the effects are small and unstable, so we will not promise weight loss.

    How to do it

    1. Sit at the table, put the phone away and turn off the screen.
    2. Look at the food, noting colour and smell.
    3. Chew the first three bites slowly, without distraction.
    4. Put the cutlery down between bites.
    5. Notice the moment when there is enough.

    Signs it is working

    • You notice that there is enough before the plate is empty.
    • The first three minutes without a phone pass without inner struggle.
    • After eating there is clarity, not heaviness and guilt.

    Time: 15-20 minutes Posture: at the table Level: beginner

    11 / Mindfulness practices

    Body scan

    A slow passage of attention from the feet to the crown of the head.

    The body scan is a basic mindfulness practice, familiar to anyone who has taken a stress reduction course. You lie or sit and move attention through the body in sequence, from the feet to the head, noting whatever is there: warmth, weight, emptiness, tingling.

    The practice grew out of MBSR, which Jon Kabat-Zinn began at the University of Massachusetts in 1979. In the eight-week course the body scan became the first and central exercise: a forty-five-minute recording done at home. The first large application to chronic pain dates to 1982.

    It is usually the first practice people meet, because it needs neither breath concentration nor imagery. The task is simple and mechanical, so the mind resists less. Many people fall asleep, and that is fine too: it means the body is genuinely resting.

    How it works

    The scan trains interoception, the perception of internal signals, and the ability to direct attention deliberately. Those two things underlie most bodily practices: without them you can neither relax nor notice anxiety in time.

    The evidence is modest. The review by Hilton and colleagues (2016) of MBSR finds small effects for chronic pain and low quality of the studies. The scan is rarely tested apart from the programme; in Sparacio and colleagues (2024) single exercises produced a small acute effect. This is a practice of habit, not a procedure.

    How to do it

    1. Lie on your back, arms along the body, eyes closed.
    2. Begin with the toes and move slowly upward.
    3. Rest in each area for two or three breaths.
    4. Do not change the sensations, simply note them.
    5. Finish at the crown and feel the body as a whole.

    Signs it is working

    • You can tell heaviness from emptiness in different parts of the body.
    • The breath is not changed on purpose, yet it deepens by itself.
    • After practice the body is audible rather than buzzing.

    Time: 10-20 minutes Posture: lying down Level: beginner

    Questions

    What people usually ask

    How long before I notice an effect?

    Usually two to four weeks of regular practice, ten to fifteen minutes a session. The first thing that changes is not the state but the ability to notice that attention has gone. If nothing shifts in a month, change the practice rather than pushing harder.

    My thoughts will not stop. Am I failing?

    No. The mind produces thoughts the way the heart beats. The task is not to stop the stream but to notice the moment you fell into it, and return. Every return is the practice.

    Do I need a teacher?

    Not for basic techniques: a clear description and regularity cover most questions. A teacher matters where strong material comes up: trauma, panic, long retreats. Then a living person nearby is worth more than any book.

    Can I practise with music or an app?

    Yes. Music and a guiding voice help hold the format, especially at the start. Eventually try at least one session in silence to feel the difference.

    Does this cure anxiety or depression?

    No. Some practices lower stress and improve mood, and research supports that. But with anxiety, depression and other conditions, meditation works together with therapy and a doctor, not instead of them.

    How is this different from rest or sleep?

    Rest restores energy, sleep repairs the body. Meditation trains attention: you learn to notice what is happening without fleeing it or fusing with it. It can be pleasant, but pleasantness is not the goal.

    Pace

    Practise at your own rhythm

    None of these practices has to be done every day, and none has to be done perfectly. It is enough to return to the one that resonates and to notice what changes in ordinary life.

    These descriptions are offered in a cultural and historical context. They are not medical advice: with health concerns, acute conditions or a psychiatric diagnosis, speak to a doctor first.
